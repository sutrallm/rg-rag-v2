**Title: The Path of Non-Attachment: Key Principles for Authentic Spiritual Practice**

**Summary:**  
True spiritual practice is defined not by the attainment of境界 (states or experiences) or external achievements, but by the consistent release of attachment to them. Practitioners must avoid clinging to any experience, including meditative states, spiritual powers, or personal accomplishments, as such attachment impedes liberation. The core of practice lies in maintaining inner freedom, cultivating wisdom and mindfulness, and directing effort toward the cessation of suffering—not the pursuit of results. Detachment from worldly pursuits such as fame, profit, and status is essential to align with the ultimate aim: liberation from suffering through the realization of impermanence, non-self, and the cessation of craving.

---

**Non-Attachment to States or Experiences Is the Foundation of Practice**  
Practitioners must not identify progress or success with the appearance of any境界, whether meditative, mystical, or emotional. Even when such states arise during practice, clinging to them—whether out of pride, desire, or fear—creates bondage. The true measure of progress is the ability to remain unshaken, free from fixation, regardless of internal or external phenomena.

**Avoiding Attachment to Outcomes Preserves Inner Freedom**  
Pursuing spiritual results is not inherently wrong, but attachment to those results—whether as milestones, validations, or goals—becomes a chain. The practice must be sustained without clinging to the fruits of effort. Liberation arises when the practitioner releases not only the desire for境界 but also the desire for the absence of them, maintaining equanimity in all conditions.

**Wisdom and Mindfulness Prevent Entanglement in Illusory States**  
Wisdom enables the practitioner to see the transient and empty nature of all境界. Mindfulness ensures continuous awareness that prevents identification with passing experiences. Together, they allow the practitioner to engage fully in practice without becoming ensnared by the allure of pleasant states or the aversion to unpleasant ones.

**Worldly Pursuits Are Obstacles to Liberation**  
Fame, profit, and social status are rooted in desire and ego. Attachment to these distracts from the true aim of practice: the cessation of suffering. Practitioners must consciously relinquish these pursuits, recognizing them as temporary and ultimately unsatisfying. Only by turning away from worldly goals can one align with the path leading to peace and freedom.

**The Ultimate Aim Is Liberation, Not Achievement**  
The goal of practice is not to accumulate experiences, powers, or accolades, but to extinguish greed, hatred, and delusion. The highest state is not marked by extraordinary phenomena, but by the absence of clinging, the stillness of mind, and the complete freedom from suffering. This is the essence of the path: to realize peace beyond all conditions.

**Mistaken Focus on States or Powers Leads Away from the True Path**  
Seeking supernatural abilities, meditative bliss, or spiritual validation is a common deviation. These pursuits divert attention from the core practice of observing reality as it is. Such fixation creates new forms of attachment and obscures the insight necessary for liberation. The true path requires relinquishing all forms of grasping—even the grasp for spiritual progress.

**Understanding Impermanence and Non-Self Releases the Mind**  
All境界 are impermanent and devoid of inherent self. Recognizing this prevents the mind from clinging to any experience as permanent or substantial. Insight into impermanence and non-self dissolves the illusion that any境界 represents true attainment, allowing the practitioner to rest in natural awareness, free from conceptual fixation.

**The Practice of Non-Attachment Is Embodied in Daily Life**  
Authentic practice is not confined to meditation sessions. It manifests in how one responds to all situations—whether mundane or profound. Practitioners must maintain equanimity, without preference or aversion, regardless of the nature of experience. The mind that remains steady and unattached in daily life is the mind that is truly liberated.

**External Validation and Internal Pride Are Traps**  
When a practitioner becomes pleased or proud of their progress or experiences, it signals the emergence of ego. This pride is an obstacle to further growth and contradicts the nature of true practice, which is humble, continuous, and free from self-reference. Liberation requires the dissolution of the sense of “I” that claims ownership over any achievement.

**The Path Requires Clear Discrimination Between Two Kinds of Goals**  
Worldly goals seek satisfaction through acquisition and status; spiritual goals seek freedom through renunciation and insight. Practitioners must clearly distinguish between these and progressively turn away from the former. Only by abandoning the pursuit of pleasure, gain, or recognition can one fully embrace the path that leads to the cessation of suffering.

**True Practice Is Marked by Compassion and Equanimity**  
A liberated practitioner is not moved by external rewards or internal states. Their actions are guided by compassion and wisdom, not by desire for recognition or results. This balance allows them to serve others without attachment, embodying the harmony of inner freedom and outer engagement.

**The Role of Death and Sensory Temptation in Practice**  
External forces—whether symbolic (like the “death demon”) or sensory—may tempt the mind to cling to pleasant experiences. The practitioner must respond with wisdom, seeing these as transient and illusory. Neither grasping nor rejecting them is necessary; instead, observing their nature without attachment allows the mind to remain free.

**The Highest State Transcends All States**  
Nirvana is not a境界 to be attained, but the cessation of all clinging. It is beyond all phenomena, including spiritual experiences. The practitioner who realizes this does not seek any state, even the most sublime, because they understand that freedom lies in non-attachment—not in the possession of any experience, however refined.
