Title: Jinjin and the Cultivation of Meditative Concentration

Summary:  
Sustained, vigilant effort—known as Jinjin—is essential for developing and preserving deep meditative concentration. It directly opposes two primary obstacles: dullness and sloth, which create mental heaviness and confusion, and scatteredness, which disperses focus. These obstructive states are not merely passive conditions but active threats to progress, requiring continuous counteraction through alertness and unwavering mindfulness. Even those who have attained deep wisdom maintain this effort without interruption, indicating that diligence is not a阶段性 step but a permanent foundation of practice.

—

Jinjin Sustains Meditative Concentration  
Continuous effort is non-negotiable for holding onto meditative concentration. Without persistent vigilance, concentration deteriorates into ignorance and desire, revealing that stability in practice depends not on fleeting achievement but on unbroken application. Jinjin functions as the sustaining force that prevents regression, regardless of prior attainment.

—

Dullness and Sloth Are Opposed by Alertness  
Mental heaviness and lack of clarity—the defining traits of dullness and sloth—are not neutral states but active barriers to meditation. They are overcome not by temporary motivation or passive intention, but by the persistent cultivation of alertness. Jinjin serves as the active countermeasure that restores mental lightness and clarity.

—

Scatteredness Is Subdued Through Continuous Attention  
Mental dispersion undermines concentration by fragmenting focus. It is not addressed through occasional redirection but by reinforcing ongoing vigilance. Jinjin’s role here is to unify attention, making sustained presence the antidote to fragmentation.

—

Even the Wise Remain Diligent  
Attaining deep wisdom does not terminate the need for diligent practice. Those who have progressed significantly—designated as Shengzhe—continue to cultivate mindfulness and diligence without pause, demonstrating that effort is not a means to an end but an enduring dimension of realized practice.
