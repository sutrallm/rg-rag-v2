**Title: Non-Attachment, No-Self, and the Nature of Liberation in Chan Practice**

**Summary:**  
True freedom arises not through external achievements or meditative states, but through the complete dissolution of clinging to self, experiences, and spiritual goals. This freedom is cultivated by observing the impermanent, interdependent, and selfless nature of all phenomena—especially the five aggregates—and by maintaining mindful awareness in ordinary life. Liberation is marked by equanimity, ethical conduct, and the absence of reactivity, craving, or identity-based delusion. The highest expression of this state is non-practicing practice: resting naturally in awareness without striving, having let go even of the notion of progress or attainment.

**Non-Attachment as the Essence of Freedom**  
Non-attachment is the natural outcome of seeing all phenomena as transient and without inherent substance. It is not passive indifference, but the absence of clinging to pleasurable, unpleasant, or even sublime meditative experiences—including the idea of liberation itself. When one no longer grasps at identities, outcomes, or spiritual attainments, the mind becomes unobstructed, equanimous, and free from pride or discouragement. This state is both the path and the culmination: clinging binds, while non-clinging is freedom. Even attachment to being a “meditator” or possessing “purity” or “supernormal powers” must be relinquished for true liberation.

**The Illusion of Self as the Root of Suffering**  
The sense of “I” and “mine” is recognized as a constructed illusion formed from the five aggregates—body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, and consciousness—which are constantly changing and interdependently arisen. When one directly observes these aggregates as impermanent and empty of inherent existence, the foundation for egoic clinging collapses. The questions “who suffers?” or “who is liberated?” dissolve, as no permanent, independent entity can be found to experience or attain anything. This realization cuts the root of suffering, ending the cycle of identity-based delusion and emotional reactivity.

**Wisdom Arises Through Direct Observation, Not Ritual**  
Liberation is not achieved through external rituals, supernatural aids, or striving for special states. It emerges from mindful observation of everyday experience—walking, sitting, eating—where one sees the arising and passing of thoughts, sensations, and feelings. This direct perception reveals impermanence and emptiness, leading naturally to the insight of no-self. Wisdom is not abstract knowledge but experiential clarity that dismantles illusion, renders clinging irrational, and makes non-attachment effortless.

**Non-Practicing Practice as the Culmination of Liberation**  
The highest form of spiritual cultivation is rest without striving, seeking, or aversion—recognizing that awareness itself is already complete and free. This state, called non-practicing practice, arises only when attachment to methods, goals, and outcomes has ceased. It is the natural expression of a mind unbound by the need to become, achieve, or maintain any condition. When even the idea of “practice” is no longer clung to, the mind abides in its innate stillness.

**Interior Liberation Through Ethical Disciplines and Mindfulness**  
True freedom is an inner condition, unshakable by external circumstances or internal thoughts. It is sustained by ethical conduct, continuous mindfulness, and non-negligence—maintaining awareness that prevents forgetfulness and the subtle return of clinging. The saintly mind is defined by its emptiness: no self-attachment, no craving, no aversion, no dependence on validation. Such a mind is boundaryless—accepting without grasping, rejecting nothing—like space, naturally realized through the absence of grasping and aversion.

**Chandong as the Means to Dissolve Clinging**  
Chandong stabilizes the mind so that insight into impermanence and no-self can be clearly seen beyond conceptual thought. It is not reached by pursuing calm or blissful states, but by remaining unmoved by disturbances—both internal and external. Through this stabilization, one observes experiences without reacting, revealing their insubstantial nature. Non-attachment is both cultivated by and required for authentic Chandong, ensuring the practice does not become another form of subtle grasping. True progress lies in letting go of meditative achievements as ends in themselves.

**The Union of Impermanence, Emptiness, and Liberation**  
All phenomena are impermanent, interdependently arisen, and devoid of inherent existence. Recognizing this emptiness dissolves the illusion of solidity in objects, experiences, and identities. Insight into impermanence directly leads to the realization of no-self, because nothing enduring or fixed can be found within shifting processes to be called “I” or “mine.” This triad—impermanence, emptiness, no-self—forms the foundation of liberating wisdom. When fully realized, it erases the root of suffering, terminates the cycle of birth and death, and manifests as inner stillness, ethical purity, and complete freedom.
