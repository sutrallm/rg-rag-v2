**Title: Chan Ding, Jing Jin, and Zhi Hui: The Unified Path to Liberation in Buddhist Practice**

**Summary:**  
True liberation arises from the inseparable integration of sustained meditative concentration (Chan Ding), unwavering diligent effort (Jing Jin), and transcendent wisdom (Zhi Hui). Chan Ding establishes mental stillness and clarity by anchoring awareness in the present moment, free from distraction and emotional turbulence. This state is not self-sustaining—it requires constant, mindful effort (Jing Jin) to develop, maintain, and deepen, with moral discipline reducing external and internal disturbances. From this stabilized mind arises Zhi Hui, the direct perception of impermanence, non-self, and interdependence in all phenomena, dissolving attachment not only to worldly objects but also to meditative experiences themselves. Without wisdom, concentration remains incomplete; without effort, both concentration and wisdom falter. Authentic practice unfolds entirely within daily life, where mindfulness transforms ordinary actions into paths of awakening, and afflictions are not suppressed but understood as signals pointing toward deeper realization.

---

**Chan Ding is the Foundation of Mental Clarity and Stillness**  
Meditative concentration arises through sustained, undistracted mindfulness anchored in the present moment. It calms mental disturbances, eliminates wandering thoughts and emotional entanglements, and produces inner peace, stability, and purity. This state is not passive stillness but an active, unified awareness that repels recklessness and delusion. It serves as the essential internal condition for deeper realization, enabling clear observation of body, mind, and external phenomena as impermanent and interdependent. However, it cannot stand alone—without wisdom and moral discipline, it remains insufficient for liberation.

**Jing Jin Sustains and Deepens Meditative Stability**  
Unwavering, continuous effort is the driving force behind the development and endurance of meditative concentration. Without persistent diligence, focus collapses into distraction, delusion, and defilements. This effort is not occasional or situational but a constant, moment-by-moment vigilance applied in every activity—walking, sitting, breathing. It counters laziness, desire, and ignorance, and prevents regression on the path. The Holy One embodies this as an unbroken habit of guarding the mind, maintaining purity, and resisting the pull of attachment. Effort is the fuel that transforms mindfulness into deep concentration and prevents its erosion.

**Zhi Hui Transforms Perception and Dissolves Attachment**  
Wisdom emerges from the stillness of meditative concentration, revealing the true nature of all experience: impermanence, non-self, and dependent origination. This understanding dismantles clinging—not only to material things but also to meditative attainments, supernatural powers, or spiritual achievements. The realization that the self is a transient aggregation of changing elements dissolves the illusion of a fixed "I," which is the root of suffering. Wisdom does not suppress afflictions but observes their arising and ceasing, recognizing their empty nature. It is through this insight that liberation is completed, as attachment to all phenomena—including the meditative state—is fully relinquished.

**The Present Moment is the Sole Ground of Practice**  
Authentic spiritual engagement occurs exclusively in the here and now. No retreat, ritual, or formal meditation substitutes for continuous awareness in daily life. Whether sitting in stillness or performing routine acts, the practitioner remains grounded in Dang Xia, observing breath, emotion, and thought without clinging to past or future. This unwavering presence—what is called daily mindfulness—transforms ordinary moments into the very arena of awakening. It is here that Chan Ding is cultivated, Jing Jin is enacted, and Zhi Hui matures.

**Moral Discipline and Effort Together Protect the Mind**  
Ethical conduct reduces the mental disturbances caused by harmful actions, creating the external and internal conditions necessary for concentration to arise and endure. Pure moral discipline diminishes remorse and distraction, allowing the mind to settle more easily. When paired with relentless effort, it forms a protective framework that shields practice from degradation. Without this foundation, even sustained concentration risks slipping into attachment or delusion, and wisdom may remain theoretical rather than transformative.

**Liberation Requires the Completion of All Three Dimensions**  
Freedom from the cycle of birth and death comes only after the complete eradication of afflictions. This is not achieved through concentration alone, nor through effort alone, nor through wisdom alone. True liberation requires their union: Chan Ding quiets the mind, Jing Jin fuels its stability, and Zhi Hui penetrates the illusions sustaining suffering. When these are fully integrated—as embodied in the framework of Jie Ding Hui—the practitioner naturally abides in tranquility, not as a temporary state, but as the unwavering ground of being.
