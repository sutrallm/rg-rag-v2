**Title: Zhichuang, Jie Tuo, and Ping Deng Xin: The Path to Liberation as Taught by Fo Tu**

**Summary**  
Liberation (Jie Tuo) is achieved only through personal practice and awareness, not through external救助. It requires transcending all forms of attachment—including spiritual experiences—through transcendent wisdom (Zhichuang), rooted in the observation of impermanence and non-self. This wisdom is expressed as equal-mindedness (Ping Deng Xin), the unshakable calm of a mind undisturbed by pleasure or pain, and compassion (Ci Bei) that arises without attachment or aversion. Fo Tu guides this path but cannot grant liberation; it must be realized by the individual through disciplined concentration (Chan Ding) and the dissolution of the three poisons.

---

**Transcendent Wisdom Arises from Observing Impermanence**  
The foundation of liberation is the clear perception that all phenomena are impermanent and lack inherent self. This direct observation dismantles clinging—not only to worldly objects, but also to meditative states, attainments, or spiritual identities. Without this seeing, even spiritual progress becomes a subtle form of attachment that prevents true freedom.

**Liberation Requires Complete Non-Clinging**  
True freedom is not found in achieving blissful states or transcendent experiences, but in releasing all forms of grasping—whether toward pleasure, pain, achievement, or even the desire for liberation itself. Any remaining attachment, even to spiritual goals, binds the mind to samsara. Liberation is defined by the absence of attachment, not by the presence of special states.

**Equal-Mindedness Is the Hallmark of the Liberated Mind**  
The liberated person does not react to circumstances with preference or aversion. Their mind remains steady like earth or space—unmoved by praise or blame, gain or loss, joy or sorrow. This equanimity is not passive indifference, but the active expression of wisdom, freeing one from the distortions of judgment and ego.

**Meditative Concentration Is the Foundation, Not the Goal**  
Stillness of mind through concentration creates the conditions for liberation, but alone it is insufficient. It must be paired with transcendent wisdom to prevent the practitioner from mistaking calm for freedom. Without wisdom, concentration may reinforce attachment to meditative bliss, trapping the mind in a refined form of craving.

**The Buddha Shows the Way, But Does Not Grant Liberation**  
Fo Tu reveals the path—through concentration, wisdom, and ethical conduct—but remains a guide, not a savior. His presence, teachings, and example provide direction, yet each individual must walk the path themselves. No ritual, prayer, or divine intervention can substitute for personal effort and sustained awareness.

**Compassion Emerges Naturally from Non-Attachment**  
Caring for others without conditions—free from love based on favoritism or hatred born of aversion—is not a separate practice, but the natural expression of wisdom. When the self is no longer the center of experience, compassion flows effortlessly, undistorted by ego or expectation.

**Liberation Is the Eradication of the Three Poisons**  
Those who have fully realized freedom have uprooted greed, hatred, and delusion. Their awareness is serene, unmoved by internal turmoil or external triggers. This state is not mystical or distant; it is the ordinary mind unclouded by clinging, fully present, and completely at peace.
