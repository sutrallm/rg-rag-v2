**Title: Yungli, Wuwo, and the Transformation of Defilements in the Path of Shengzhe**

**Summary:**  
The path to liberation centers on the cultivation of yungli—transcendent wisdom rooted in the direct observation of impermanence and non-self. This wisdom dismantles the three root defilements: chi (fundamental delusion), chen (anger), and gan (greed). Shengzhe, having fully realized wuwo (non-self), no longer experiences these afflictions, as they lose their foundation when the illusion of a permanent self is extinguished. Practices such as chanding (mental stillness) and xiuwei (continuous non-judgmental observation) support the arising of yungli, which in turn dissolves attachment, aversion, and ignorance by revealing the transient and selfless nature of all experience.

**Yungli as the Unifying Force of Liberation**  
Yungli emerges through sustained observation of impermanence and non-self, allowing direct perception of reality without distortion. It dismantles the core misconceptions that sustain suffering: the belief in permanence and the illusion of a fixed self. This wisdom does not merely suppress defilements but uproots them at their source by revealing their groundlessness. It is not an intellectual understanding but a lived realization that transforms perception and reaction.

**Wuwo as the Annihilation of Self-Identity**  
The direct realization of non-self (wuwo) eliminates the foundation upon which all defilements depend. Chi, the mistaken belief in a permanent self, collapses when wuwo is seen. Chen, which arises from protecting a vulnerable sense of “I,” vanishes because there is no “I” to defend. Gan, fueled by clinging to “mine,” dissolves when the object of attachment—self—is recognized as illusory. Wuwo is not an abstract concept but the experiential unraveling of the very structure that gives rise to suffering.

**The Interdependence of Chi, Wu Ming, and the Defilements**  
Chi (delusion) and wu ming (ignorance) are synonymous, representing the foundational confusion about reality—particularly the misperception of self and permanence. This confusion is the origin of all other disturbances. Chen and gan arise from chi: aversion emerges in resistance to what threatens the false self, while greed clings to what it believes will satisfy it. Without chi, these secondary afflictions cannot sustain themselves. Thus, dispelling chi through wuwo renders chen and gan powerless.

**Chanding and Xiuwei as Supports for Realization**  
Mental stillness (chanding) creates the conditions necessary for yungli to arise, removing the agitation that obscures perception. Continuous, non-judgmental observation (xiuwei) in daily life nurtures this wisdom by training attention on the arising and passing of all phenomena. Neither practice alone produces liberation, but together they stabilize awareness and deepen the direct seeing that undermines illusion.

**Impermanence as the Key to Dissolving Aversion and Greed**  
Observing impermanence (wu chang) reveals that all states—pleasant or unpleasant—are transient. This understanding weakens chen by showing that resistance to pain is futile, as no unpleasant condition endures. It weakens gan by exposing the futility of clinging to pleasure, since it inevitably decays. Wu chang does not demand control or suppression; it invites surrender to reality as it is, thereby dissolving the reactive patterns that bind beings to suffering.

**Shengzhe as the Embodiment of Transcendence**  
Shengzhe has fully extinguished chi, chen, and gan not through force but through wisdom. These defilements are absent not because they were suppressed, but because their basis—the delusion of self—has been dismantled. Shengzhe exists in continuous awareness of truth, unshaken by experience, because yungli has revealed the empty, conditioned, and selfless nature of all phenomena. There is no longer a separate entity to be harmed, pleased, or threatened.
