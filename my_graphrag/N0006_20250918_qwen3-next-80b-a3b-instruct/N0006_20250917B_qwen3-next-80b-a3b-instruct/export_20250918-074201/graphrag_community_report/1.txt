**Title: Jingjin, Chanding, and Stillness: The Path to Liberation Through Diligent Practice and Meditative Stability**

**Summary:**  
Liberation from the cycle of birth and death is achieved exclusively through relentless, moment-to-moment effort (Jingjin), which sustains meditative concentration (Chanding) and leads to inner stillness. Chanding, rooted in continuous mindfulness and ethical discipline, calms the mind, weakens craving and aversion, and enables clear perception of impermanence and non-self. Without sustained diligent practice, even the most profound meditative states decay into slackness, re-entangling the practitioner in ignorance and suffering. True freedom is not found in rituals or transient peace, but in the unwavering alignment of ethical conduct, vigilant awareness, and non-attached wisdom across all activities.

---

**Diligent Effort (Jingjin) is the Indispensable Foundation for Liberation**  
Continuous, unwavering exertion is the only means to overcome ignorance, desire, laziness, and habitual patterns that bind beings to samsara. Without this persistent practice, spiritual progress regresses, meditation fades, and the mind slips back into distraction and complacency. Jingjin is not occasional or ceremonial—it is relentless, applied in every thought, action, and breath, whether sitting in meditation or walking, eating, or feeling anger. It directly counters sloth and heedlessness, sustaining mindfulness and preventing regression. It is the active force that transforms teaching into realization and is the sole pathway to becoming a saintly being.

---

**Meditative Concentration (Chanding) Stabilizes the Mind to Reveal True Nature**  
Chanding is not mere focus—it is deep, unmoving mental stability cultivated through sustained mindfulness and ethical discipline. It arises only when the mind is anchored in the present moment, free from craving, aversion, and distraction. This concentration calms internal agitation such as greed and anger, quiets mental chatter, and creates the clarity needed to perceive impermanence, non-self, and unsatisfactoriness. However, Chanding alone is insufficient; without wisdom to prevent attachment to meditative states, it becomes another form of bondage. It is the necessary condition for insight, and its depth depends entirely on the strength of diligent effort.

---

**Stillness is the Natural Outcome of Non-Attachment and Clear Perception**  
Stillness is not a goal to be pursued, but the natural result of removing mental disturbances through sustained practice. It is likened to still water—undisturbed by external conditions or internal reactions. This inner peace emerges when craving, aversion, and identification with self dissolve under the clear light of mindfulness and wisdom. A saintly being abides in this stillness perpetually, unaffected by pleasure or pain, success or failure. It is not passive calm, but the active absence of clinging, demonstrating that true tranquility comes not from suppressing experience, but from seeing it as it is—impermanent, selfless, and ungraspable.

---

**Mindfulness in Daily Life is the Living Expression of Spiritual Practice**  
True cultivation is not confined to formal meditation. It is manifested in every activity—walking, sitting, eating, speaking—through uninterrupted awareness. This continuous presence (Jiaochaohu, Richi shixing, Zhengnian) is the practical extension of Chanding into ordinary life, ensuring that mental stability is not temporary but ingrained. When mindfulness permeates daily actions, it prevents lapse into dullness, distraction, and indulgence. It transforms routine moments into opportunities for insight, making liberation accessible at all times. Without this integration, practice becomes isolated and ineffective.

---

**Transcendent Wisdom (Weizhi) Arises from the Union of Concentration and Diligence**  
Wisdom is not abstract knowledge but direct realization of impermanence, non-self, and unsatisfactoriness. It emerges only when the mind, stabilized by Chanding and energized by Jingjin, observes phenomena without clinging or aversion. This wisdom dissolves attachment not by force, but by seeing through the illusion of permanence and identity. It renders meditative states harmless by removing the desire to possess them. Wisdom deepens Chanding by preventing spiritual materialism, and Chanding enables wisdom by providing the clarity to perceive reality unfiltered. Their mutual reinforcement is essential.

---

**Ethical Conduct Provides the Necessary Ground for Mental Stability**  
Moral discipline (Sila, Jie) is the indispensable foundation upon which Chanding and mindfulness are built. Without purity of action and speech, the mind remains agitated by guilt, remorse, or sensory craving, making concentration unstable. Ethical conduct shields the six sense bases from overstimulation, creating an inner environment where stillness can arise naturally. It is not separate from meditation but its precondition: one cannot attain deep concentration while indulging in harm, deceit, or sensual excess.

---

**Non-Attachment is the Culmination of Practice, Not Its Byproduct**  
True freedom is not the absence of thought, desire, or emotion—but the absence of identification with them. This non-attachment (Wuzhu, Wuzhizhidi) is cultivated by observing all experiences—pleasant or unpleasant—as transient, not as “mine.” It prevents even the most refined meditative states from becoming objects of clinging. The pinnacle of realization is the non-abiding mind: undisturbed by any condition, including peace or bliss. Without this detachment, the practitioner remains entangled in a subtler form of samsara, mistaking calm for liberation.

---

**The Saintly Being Embodies Perfection in Effort, Concentration, and Stillness**  
A saintly being is defined not by titles or rituals, but by the seamless integration of constant effort, unwavering concentration, and unshakeable stillness. Their mind remains calm regardless of circumstance, their actions arise from awareness, and their presence reflects freedom from greed, anger, and delusion. This state is not attained through isolated bursts of practice, but through lifelong, unbroken diligence. It is the natural fruit of uninterrupted mindfulness, disciplined conduct, and radiant insight—manifested in every breath, every step, every moment of ordinary life.
