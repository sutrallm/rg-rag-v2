**Title: Zhihui, Jie, and Jiexie: The Triadic Path to Liberation in Buddhist Practice**

**Summary**  
Liberation arises through the inseparable integration of ethical conduct (Jie), meditative concentration (Chanding), and liberating wisdom (Zhihui). Zhihui, the direct experiential realization of impermanence, suffering, and non-self, dissolves clinging to all phenomena—including spiritual attainments—and leads to complete freedom from suffering (Jiexie). Jie provides the moral and mental stability required for Chanding to take root; Chanding, in turn, creates the clarity necessary for Zhihui to arise. Without Jie, practice becomes unstable; without Zhihui, even deep concentration remains bound to samsara. Liberation is not attained through textual authority, celestial power, or escapism, but through personal, diligent practice grounded in mindfulness of daily life and the direct insight that all phenomena are empty of inherent existence. The highest expression of this path is the state of Shengzhe—a being whose conduct, concentration, and wisdom are perfectly unified, whose mind rests in non-duality, and who sees worldly afflictions as the very ground of awakening.

---

**Ethical Conduct is the Unshakable Base of Spiritual Progress**  
Moral discipline (Jie) governs body, speech, and mind through intentional restraint from harm and cultivation of purity. It is not external compliance but inner guarding that stabilizes the mind, preventing agitation and delusion. Without this foundation, meditative concentration cannot develop, and insight becomes speculative or corrupt. Jie purifies the conditions for wisdom by reducing mental disturbances, allowing the mind to settle into stillness. It is the first pillar of the threefold training and is synonymous with right action in daily life. Ethical conduct is not restrictive but protective—shaping a mind capable of sustained observation and free from the disruptions of craving and aversion.

**Meditative Concentration Enables Clear Seeing**  
Concentration (Chanding) provides the sustained, undistracted mental clarity necessary for direct observation of reality. It is not an end in itself but the essential platform upon which wisdom arises. Through Chanding, the practitioner observes the impermanent, unsatisfactory, and selfless nature of phenomena with precision. This stability is cultivated by Jie and deepens through continuous effort (Jingjin). Without it, insight remains fragmented or illusory. Chanding and Zhihui mutually reinforce each other: concentration allows wisdom to penetrate, and wisdom itself refines concentration by revealing the true nature of the mind’s objects. Together, they form a dynamic cycle leading beyond temporary states to irreversible transformation.

**Liberating Wisdom Is the Direct Path to Freedom**  
Wisdom (Zhihui) is not intellectual knowledge but the direct, non-conceptual realization that all conditioned phenomena are impermanent, unsatisfactory, and without self. It arises from observing the five aggregates, the Four Foundations of Mindfulness, and dependent origination as they are experienced in the present moment. This insight dissolves the illusion of permanence, ownership, and personal identity, severing the roots of craving and ignorance. Zhihui reveals emptiness (Yuanqi Xingkong)—that nothing exists independently—and leads to non-abiding (Wuzhu), where even liberation is not clung to. It is the only means to realize Nirvana, transcend samsara, and attain the state of Shengzhe. Wisdom transforms suffering into awakening by meeting all experience—pleasure, pain, desire, and delusion—with clear awareness.

**Liberation Is the Natural Outcome of Wisdom’s Dissolution of Clinging**  
Freedom (Jiexie) is not a state to be attained externally but the cessation of all attachment, aversion, and delusion—the complete unraveling of the cycle of birth and death. It is the natural result of Zhihui’s insight into non-self and emptiness. When clinging dissolves, so does the mechanism of suffering. This state, described as a mind like empty space (Zhongjie shixiang), is beyond dualities—neither bound by samsara nor attached to nirvana. Liberation is marked by the extinction of the three poisons and the absence of conceptual grasping. It is not a reward but the unmasking of what was always already free, revealed through the unwavering practice of Jie, Chanding, and Zhihui.

**Awakening Is Cultivated in Daily Life, Not Escaped From**  
Mindfulness is not confined to formal meditation but extends into every activity—eating, walking, breathing, thinking—through Riyong. This continuous awareness transforms ordinary life into the path of awakening. The teaching that “worldly troubles are the path” (Chenlao ji daochang) affirms that liberation emerges not by fleeing experience but by meeting it with wisdom. Desire, anger, and confusion are not obstacles to be avoided but opportunities for insight when observed without reaction. This transforms the fabric of daily existence into a sustained practice of non-attachment and clarity.

**True Realization Must Be Personally Verified**  
Liberation cannot be inherited from texts, teachers, or divine intervention (Ziying). It requires direct, personal experience and experiential verification. Even heavenly beings, despite their blessings, remain within samsara and revere those who transcend through wisdom, not supernatural feats. The deepest truths—such as emptiness, non-self, and dependent origination—are known only when they are seen for oneself. Faith in doctrine is insufficient; insight must be lived, felt, and confirmed through the consistent application of ethical conduct, concentration, and mindful observation.

**The Saintly State Is Defined by Perfect Integration**  
The Shengzhe embodies the full realization of the threefold path: flawless moral conduct (Jie), unwavering concentration (Chanding), and unobstructed wisdom (Zhihui). Their ethical behavior is not forced but natural—an expression of inner freedom. Their mind is undisturbed by conditions because they perceive all phenomena as empty and transient. They have extinguished the three poisons and do not cling to enlightenment, spiritual experiences, or even the notion of liberation. They are defined not by power or charisma, but by the quiet, radical clarity that sees reality without distortion.

**Death Is a Catalyst for Awakening, Not an End**  
Contemplation of death (Siling) is not morbid but liberating. Recognizing death as a natural part of dependent origination deepens urgency (Jingjin) and guards against spiritual complacency. For the true practitioner, death is not a termination but a mirror reflecting the impermanence of all things. Awareness of mortality sharpens mindfulness, reinforces the value of every moment, and turns attention toward what truly matters—the dissolution of clinging. It is not feared but used as a guide to deepen practice and realize the freedom that lies beyond birth and death.

**The Refining Process of Spiritual Cultivation**  
The transformation of the mind is likened to gold refining: Jie is the fire that purifies impurities, Chanding is the hammer that shapes the mind through stillness, and Zhihui is the artisan who directs the process with discernment. Each element is indispensable. Without fire, the metal remains unrefined; without hammering, the form cannot emerge; without the artisan’s insight, the process lacks direction. This metaphor underscores that spiritual maturation is active, disciplined, and guided by wisdom—not passive or accidental. It is a gradual, intentional unpeeling of delusion to reveal the mind’s innate luminosity.
