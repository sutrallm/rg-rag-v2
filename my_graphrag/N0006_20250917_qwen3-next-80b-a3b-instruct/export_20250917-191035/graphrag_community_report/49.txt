**Title: Wuzhi, Chizhou, and Jiede: The Path to Liberation Through Non-Attachment**

**Summary**  
Liberation is realized not through external rituals, supernatural powers, or spiritual achievements, but through direct, mindful observation of reality. True freedom arises when one sees all phenomena as impermanent, selfless, and unsatisfactory, and lets go of clinging—even to meditative states and enlightenment itself. This state is cultivated by observing mental impulses without reaction, stabilizing the mind through concentration, and dissolving the illusion of a fixed self. Liberation is not a distant goal but a present-moment realization, lived in complete non-seeking and non-rejection.

**Non-Attachment as the Foundation of Liberation**  
Freedom from suffering is defined by the absence of grasping or rejecting any experience—whether pleasurable, painful, spiritual, or mundane. This state, known as Wuzhi, emerges not from force or suppression, but from clear seeing. It is sustained by continuous mindfulness that allows phenomena to arise and pass without interference. The absence of desire for outcomes, termed Wulian, is not passive indifference but active non-reliance on experiences for peace, and is essential to true liberation.

**Wisdom of Impermanence and Non-Self Removes Suffering**  
The core of liberating understanding lies in directly perceiving the transient and selfless nature of all phenomena. Chizhou, the wisdom of impermanence, non-self, and unsatisfactoriness, dismantles the fabric of craving by revealing that nothing—thoughts, states, achievements, or even spiritual milestones—can be held as stable or truly “mine.” This perception erodes egoic clinging, especially the belief in a permanent self (Wuwo), which is the root of all bondage. Seeing this truth dismantles the cycle of karma and rebirth at its origin.

**Concentration Enables Clear Seeing, Not Suppression**  
Stable mental focus, called Chanding, is not an end in itself but the necessary ground for insight. Through concentrated mindfulness, one observes Niantou—mental impulses without reacting to them. This prevents karmic momentum from arising. The practice is not about stopping thoughts, but about witnessing them with equanimity, allowing the mind to rest in what is, free from distortion. Without this steadiness, insight into impermanence and non-self cannot arise.

**Spiritual Attainments Can Become Obstacles**  
Even enlightenment, purity, and spiritual achievements (Shenguo) are not exempt from the requirement of non-clinging. When these are pursued or clung to, they become sources of pride and bondage, contradicting the very essence of liberation. True freedom requires abandonment of all outcomes, including the desire to be free. This includes rejecting external forms of practice (Daoxiang)—rituals, fame, powers, or material gains—as distractions from the inner observation that leads to truth.

**Liberation Is the Direct Realization of the Unborn and Undying**  
Jiede, the cessation of craving and the end of samsaric bondage, is not a future state to be attained, but the present recognition of reality as it is—beyond birth, death, and conceptual identity. This is the realization of Wuzisheng, the unborn and undying nature of existence, accessible only through non-attachment. One who embodies this state (Shengzhe) has fully seen through egoic illusions, rests in Wuzhi, and manifests Wuzhang—wisdom that is free from all defilements, even the subtle attachment to spiritual progress.

**Mindful Observation Is the Only Path**  
All pathways to liberation converge on inner observation. By observing thoughts without judgment, one cuts the chain of karma. By seeing the impermanence of all experience, one stops seeking. By recognizing the absence of a fixed self, one releases clinging. There is no shortcut, no external authority, and no mystical experience that replaces direct, non-reactive awareness. Liberation is the natural result of seeing clearly—and remaining unattached to what is seen.
