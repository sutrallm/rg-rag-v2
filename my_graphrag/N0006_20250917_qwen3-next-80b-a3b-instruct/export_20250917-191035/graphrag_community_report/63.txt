**Title: The Return to the Unaltered Mind Through Chanding and Zhengnian**

**Summary**  
Meditation aims to restore the mind to its inherent state—unchanged, undisturbed, and naturally present. This return is achieved through Chanding, the method of resting in the natural state, and Zhengnian, the clarifying awareness that dispels mental obscurations. The dissolution of illusions and attachments, particularly the notion of self, reveals the mind’s true qualities: unconditioned, luminous, and free.

**Resting in the Natural State Through Chanding**  
Chanding serves as the direct practice by which one aligns with the unaltered mind. It is not an effort to change or improve the mind, but to settle into its baseline state—free from addition or subtraction, movement or stillness. Through sustained Chanding, the mind ceases to fabricate or resist, allowing its innate stillness and radiance to emerge without interference.

**Clarity Through Zhengnian**  
Zhengnian functions as the stabilizing force that dispels mental clouding. It does not impose truth but gently removes distortions—habitual thoughts, emotional reactivity, and conceptual clinging—so the mind’s natural clarity becomes visible. Its role is not to create insight, but to reveal what was always there by clearing away what obscures it.

**The Dissolution of Self As the Path to Luminosity**  
The recognition of no-self is not an intellectual conclusion but a lived shift that dissolves the illusion of a separate, enduring identity. When this illusion dissolves, the mind’s inherent qualities—unconditioned, luminous, and unbounded—arise spontaneously. There is no need to attain or construct these qualities; they are revealed only when the structures that conceal them are seen through.

**The Unaltered Mind as the Ever-Present Aim**  
The ultimate aim of meditation is neither transformation nor attainment, but recognition: abiding in the mind as it is—never created, never destroyed, untouched by purity or defilement. This state is not distant or rare, but ever-present, obscured only by habitual patterns. The practices of Chanding and Zhengnian are not means to reach something new, but ways to cease obscuring what has always been.
