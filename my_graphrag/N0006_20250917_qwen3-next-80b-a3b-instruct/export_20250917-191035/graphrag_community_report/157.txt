**Title: Saints, Delusion, and Liberation in Buddhist Practice**

**Summary:**  
Saints are individuals who have completely eradicated greed, hatred, and delusion through sustained ethical conduct, meditative stillness, and transcendent wisdom. Their state is marked by unshakable inner peace, freedom from attachment to all experiences—including spiritual attainments—and the absence of reactive aversion or craving. Liberation from the cycle of birth and death arises only when these three root afflictions are extinguished, primarily through direct insight into impermanence, non-self, and conditioned existence. This wisdom dismantles the illusion of a permanent self and the false perception of inherent satisfaction in phenomena, leading to renunciation, non-attachment, and the cessation of suffering. Moral discipline and diligent mindfulness form the essential foundation upon which this transformation is built, and even celestial beings reverence those who have transcended all dualities.

**Saints embody complete freedom from the three root afflictions**  
Saints are defined by the total uprooting of greed, hatred, and delusion—three interdependent afflictions that sustain suffering and rebirth. Their minds are no longer driven by craving for pleasure, aversion to pain, or ignorance of reality’s true nature. This eradication is not theoretical but experiential, resulting from prolonged ethical discipline, meditative stability, and direct insight. As a consequence, they remain undisturbed by internal emotions or external conditions, radiating equanimity, stillness, and clarity. Their liberation is marked by the absence of self-identification, desire for spiritual rewards, or resistance to unpleasant experiences.

**Delusion is the foundational cause of cyclic existence**  
Delusion—rooted in ignorance of impermanence, non-self, and interdependent origination—is the underlying source of all suffering. It manifests as the mistaken belief in permanence, a fixed self, and inherent satisfaction in phenomena. This misperception gives rise to attachment and aversion, binding beings to the cycle of birth and death. Delusion perpetuates greed by making transient objects seem lasting and desirable, and fuels hatred by distorting perception of threats. Without dispelling this ignorance, liberation remains impossible. Its removal requires more than intellectual understanding; it demands direct, sustained contemplation of reality as it is.

**Liberation arises solely through transcendent wisdom**  
True liberation is not earned through ritual, status, or even meditative bliss, but through wisdom that sees through all illusions—especially the illusion of self. This wisdom reveals the empty, impermanent, and conditioned nature of all phenomena, including spiritual experiences. It enables the mind to relinquish attachment to worldly desires and even to nirvana itself. As this insight deepens, it dismantles identifications with “I” and “mine,” dissolves craving, and extinguishes aversion. Liberation is not a state to be grasped but the natural result of seeing things without distortion, leading to non-clinging and complete inner freedom.

**Moral discipline and mindful awareness are indispensable foundations**  
Ethical conduct provides the necessary stability for mental purification and the growth of wisdom. Without moral discipline, the mind remains agitated and distracted, unable to sustain the clarity required for insight. Mindful awareness—the continuous observation of body and mind—is both the practice and the outcome of inner stillness. It prevents indulgence, counters delusion, and preserves vigilance. Together, these two pillars create the conditions in which transcendent wisdom can arise and take root. Neither concentration nor wisdom can flourish without this ethical and observational groundwork.

**Greed and hatred are expressions of delusion, not isolated afflictions**  
Greed and hatred are not independent forces but secondary manifestations of delusion. Greed emerges when ignorance leads one to believe that fleeting experiences can bring lasting satisfaction; hatred arises when the same ignorance interprets impermanent conditions as threatening to a falsely perceived self. Both are sustained by the illusion of permanence and selfhood. Their cessation is not achieved through suppression but through wisdom that sees their basis as empty. As delusion is dispelled, craving naturally cools and aversion dissolves—not by force, but by the realization that nothing can be truly clung to or rejected with lasting effect.

**The cessation of suffering requires renunciation of all clinging**  
Suffering is not eliminated by changing external circumstances but by releasing all forms of attachment—even to spiritual achievements, meditative states, or the idea of liberation itself. Renunciation is not ascetic denial but the mature letting go that follows the clear understanding of impermanence and non-self. Those who attain freedom do not pursue purity or peace as goals; they simply abide in reality as it is, without grasping or rejecting. This non-attachment is not passive indifference but the active freedom of a mind unbound by desire or aversion, fully at ease with the way things are.

**Diligence is the constant safeguard against regression**  
Spiritual progress is vulnerable to indolence, complacency, and the seduction of pleasure—whether worldly or meditative. The path demands sustained, vigilant effort. Without consistent practice, the mind slips back into distraction, ignorance, and craving. This effort is not driven by fear or ambition but by clear recognition of the urgency of awakening. Death is certain, time is uncertain, and wasting life in negligence leads to spiritual bankruptcy. Diligence protects the progress made through discipline and wisdom, ensuring that insight deepens and does not fade.

**Saintly qualities arise only through integrated practice**  
The state of sainthood is not the accumulation of virtues but the unified expression of ethical conduct, meditative stability, and wisdom. These three pillars—often called the Threefold Training—are inseparable. Moral discipline calms the external mind, concentration deepens observation, and wisdom transforms perception. They work together: without discipline, concentration is unstable; without concentration, wisdom lacks clarity; without wisdom, discipline and concentration remain superficial. True saintly character emerges only when all three are perfected, resulting in a mind that is pure, still, and utterly free.
