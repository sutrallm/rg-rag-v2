**Title: The Path of the Sage: Liberation Through Non-Attachment, Wisdom, and the Eradication of the Three Poisons**

**Summary:**  
The enlightened being, known as Sheng Zhe, embodies complete freedom from suffering by uprooting greed, hatred, and delusion — the Three Poisons that bind beings to the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. This liberation is not achieved through external rituals or blessings, but through disciplined internal practice rooted in moral conduct, meditative concentration, and direct insight into impermanence and non-self. The sage lives in unshakable stillness, free from clinging to pleasure, pain, or even spiritual attainments. Their awareness is continuous, grounded in moment-to-moment mindfulness, and their actions arise from wisdom and compassion unmarred by ego. True freedom is not a future destination but is fully present when the mind no longer identifies with the five aggregates or clings to any experience as “mine.” The Buddha taught this path as one of self-reliance — liberation is innate, revealed through truthful observation, not granted from outside.

**Non-Attachment Is the Embodiment of Liberation**  
The wise person does not cling to pleasant or unpleasant experiences, nor to meditative states or spiritual achievements. This freedom arises not from suppression, but from directly seeing that all phenomena — thoughts, emotions, sensations, and even awakening experiences — are transient and without inherent substance. To be liberated is to stop grasping at “self” or “mine,” allowing the mind to rest as open and unbound as space. This is not passive indifference, but active non-clinging, cultivated through observing how attachment fuels suffering. Even the most refined meditative states are not endpoints to be possessed, but passing conditions to be witnessed without identification.

**The Three Poisons Are the Root of Suffering and Rebirth**  
Greed, hatred, and delusion — known as Ji e or San Du — are not mere emotions, but fundamental distortions that perpetuate the cycle of existence. Greed drives clinging to sensory pleasures and spiritual experiences; hatred arises from resistance to discomfort and impermanence; delusion is the mistaken belief in a permanent, independent self. These poisons feed one another: delusion creates the illusion of a fixed identity, which leads to craving for what supports it and aversion to what threatens it. The sage has completely extinguished these three, not through force, but through insight that reveals their emptiness. Without them, karmic flow ceases, and the cycle of birth and death is broken.

**Wisdom Arises from Direct Observation of Impermanence and Non-Self**  
True understanding is not intellectual but experiential, cultivated through sustained contemplation of how all things — the body, feelings, thoughts, and consciousness — continuously arise and pass away. The five aggregates, mistaken for a solid “self,” are seen clearly as conditionally assembled, interdependent, and transient. This insight dismantles the core illusion that gives rise to ego-clinging. Realizing impermanence naturally leads to the realization of non-self — if nothing lasts, nothing can be truly “me” or “mine.” This wisdom is not abstract philosophy; it is the direct result of mindfulness in daily life (Jue cha), where every breath becomes an opportunity to see things as they are.

**Ethical Conduct and Meditative Stability Are the Foundation for Insight**  
Moral discipline is not a set of external rules, but the necessary groundwork for inner clarity. Abstaining from harmful actions creates the mental calm required for deep concentration. Without purity of conduct, the mind remains agitated, making insight impossible. Concentration (Chang Ding) then stabilizes awareness, allowing the mind to settle into one-pointed focus. But concentration alone is inert without wisdom guiding it — it must be directed by the understanding of impermanence and non-self to avoid becoming spiritually complacent or attached to stillness. True meditative absorption is not marked by bliss, but by the absence of striving and clinging.

**Liberation Is Present in This Moment, Not a Future Goal**  
The awakened state (Sheng Jie) is not attained in some distant future or after death; it is revealed in the here and now through unwavering vigilance in everyday awareness. The sage’s every movement, breath, and perception is an expression of this freedom — because they no longer identify with the stories of “I,” “mine,” or “what should be.” This presence arises from Jue cha — moment-to-moment observation of phenomena as they arise and dissolve, without reaction. When the mind ceases to chase, resist, or cling, stillness (Zhen Zhen Jing Ji) naturally emerges. Liberation, then, is not something to be acquired, but the natural state uncovered when ignorance is dissolved.

**True Freedom Comes from Letting Go, Not from Accumulation**  
Inner freedom (Zi You) is not found in acquiring better experiences, deeper states, or moral superiority. It emerges solely from the relinquishment of demands on reality — whether for pleasure, stability, identity, or validation. The ego constructs a narrative of continuity and control, but this is false. When the illusion of “Wo” (self) is seen through, attachment dissolves, and with it, the suffering it produces. The sage does not need to feel anything in particular to be free — they are unshaken by gain or loss, praise or blame, because their sense of being does not depend on conditions. This is the essence of Wu Yu: the mind, like space, holds everything without being stained by it.

**The Buddha’s Teaching Is a Path of Inner Realization, Not External Ritual**  
The awakened teacher showed the way, but emphasized that no one can walk it for another. Liberation requires no external blessings, chants, or rituals. It arises only through diligent, honest practice — observing the mind, living ethically, and calming the senses. Right view (Zheng Jian) must precede all meditation; without seeing reality clearly — impermanence, non-self, dependent origination — even deep concentration becomes another form of clinging. The path is not about becoming someone better, but about seeing through the false identity that has been the source of all suffering. Wisdom is not inherited or bestowed; it is uncovered within, by those who practice with unrelenting effort (Jing Jin Xiu Xing).
