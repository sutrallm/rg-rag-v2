**Title: Inner Peace, Letting Go, and the Middle Way in Chan Ding Practice**

**Summary:**  
Inner peace is the stable mental state achieved through Chan Ding practice, rooted in mindfulness, wisdom, and the persistent application of right effort. This state emerges only when all attachments—whether to pleasure, suffering, status, or even spiritual attainment—are released in the present moment. The Middle Way serves as the balanced path, avoiding indulgence and asceticism, and is expressed through non-clinging awareness of transient phenomena. Liberation is not a future goal but a present realization, made possible by continuous vigilance, direct observation of the now, and the active dissolution of identification with self and external objects. Saintly practitioners are defined by their enduring inner peace, forged through unwavering commitment to these principles.

**The Present Moment Is the Sole Ground for Practice and Release**  
All spiritual progress occurs exclusively in the now. Neither future conditions nor ideal environments enable transformation. True letting go happens only when one recognizes the impermanence of attachments in real time. Right effort must be sustained continuously in this moment to maintain mindfulness and prevent mental distraction. There is no pathway outside the immediate awareness of thought and sensation.

**Letting Go Is the Active Release of Attachment and Identification**  
Freedom arises not from acquisition but from the deliberate abandonment of clinging to wealth, relationships, achievements, or even spiritual progress. This is not passive resignation but an active mental discipline that dissolves identification with self and possessions. By relinquishing control and resistance, the mind is freed from agitation and suffering. This release is the direct mechanism that erases the root of bondage and enables inner peace.

**The Middle Way Embodies Non-Attachment Through Balanced Awareness**  
The path avoids both indulgence in sensual pleasures and extreme self-denial. It is expressed through equal restraint from grasping and rejecting experience, relying instead on clear, non-reactive awareness of all phenomena. This balance is not a compromise but a profound alignment with reality as it is—transient, conditioned, and devoid of inherent self. It is practiced through mindful observation of the senses and mental activity, without reliance on rituals or external forces.

**Right Effort Sustains Mental Stability Through Continuous Vigilance**  
Achieving and maintaining calm concentration demands unrelenting attention. Sloth, ignorance, and distraction are prevented not by occasional discipline but by persistent, moment-to-moment awareness. Right effort is the necessary force that upholds mindfulness, ensuring the mind does not lapse into agitation or delusion. It is the silent guard that protects the space where inner peace arises.

**Inner Peace Is the Natural Outcome of Successful Practice**  
When mental stillness is consistently cultivated through Chan Ding, agitation, greed, and confusion naturally dissolve. This state is not forced or manufactured—it arises as a byproduct of letting go, right effort, and present-moment awareness. Those who have fully mastered these principles are marked by a deep, unshakable calm, indicating spiritual maturity.

**Non-Self Is Revealed Through Direct Observation of Impermanence**  
By observing mental and physical phenomena in the present moment with clarity, one sees their fleeting, conditioned nature. There is no fixed, permanent self to be found—only ever-changing processes. This realization leads inevitably to the release of attachment, as there is nothing substantial to hold onto. The understanding of non-self is not conceptual but experiential, forming the foundation for true liberation.
