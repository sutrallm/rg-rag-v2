**Title: The Threefold Training and the Path to Liberation in Buddhist Practice**

**Summary**  
The systematic path of Jiedinghui — moral discipline, meditative concentration, and wisdom — is the only means to achieve complete liberation from suffering. Each component is indispensable and interdependent: discipline purifies action and calms the mind, concentration stabilizes attention to enable clear perception, and wisdom directly dismantles ignorance by revealing the impermanent, selfless, and empty nature of all phenomena. Liberation arises not through external rites or divine aid but through inner transformation, characterized by the total cessation of clinging — even to spiritual achievements. The final state of freedom, Nierhua, is marked by non-dual awareness, freedom from craving and aversion, and the absence of attachment to any notion of attainment. True realization is embodied in the Shengzhe, who have fully unified these elements and no longer seek, reject, or identify with any experience.

---

**Moral Discipline as the Foundational Purifier**  
Moral discipline (Jie) establishes the basis for all progress by purifying behavior and eliminating regret, which otherwise destabilizes the mind. Without this grounding, the cultivation of concentration becomes unreliable. It is not a mere set of rules but the first active step in creating the internal conditions necessary for deeper practice. Its role is structural: it precedes and supports concentration, making the mind receptive to insight.

**Meditative Concentration as the Central Bridge**  
Meditative concentration (Ding), known as Chanding, serves as the pivotal link between ethical conduct and liberating wisdom. It is neither an end nor a standalone practice but the stable mental platform required for direct observation of reality. Through sustained focus, the mind becomes clear and unwavering, enabling the emergence of insight into the true nature of phenomena. Its effectiveness depends entirely on the prior establishment of discipline and its necessity for the arising of wisdom.

**Wisdom as the Culminating Force**  
Wisdom (Hui), embodied in Chengzhi and Chizhe, is the transformative power that dissolves ignorance by perceiving the impermanent, suffering, and selfless nature of all things. It does not arise from intellectual understanding alone but from direct meditative observation sustained by concentration. This wisdom functions as the antidote to craving, aversion, and delusion — it sees through illusions of permanence and selfhood that fuel suffering, and it alone can sever the root causes of rebirth and dissatisfaction.

**Complete Letting Go as the Marker of Liberation**  
True freedom is defined by She li yiqie — the total relinquishment of all attachments, including those to meditative states, spiritual attainments, or the idea of progress itself. Any clinging, even subtle, impedes ultimate liberation. This is not passive indifference but active non-identification with experience. The Shengzhe exemplify this state, having moved beyond all dualistic striving and no longer identifying with any achievement or stage of practice.

**Emptiness as the Object of Insight**  
The insight into Wuzixing — the empty, dependently originated nature of phenomena — is central to liberating wisdom. All experiences, including the five aggregates (Wuyun), are seen as lacking independent essence. The illusion of a fixed self is the source of suffering; recognizing its illusory nature removes the ground upon which attachment, aversion, and delusion arise. This understanding is not theoretical but experiential, cultivated through sustained mindfulness and concentration.

**Non-Attachment to Attainment as the Final Ethical Stance**  
Buzhi guocheng — the refusal to cling to any notion of gain — is not a step beyond wisdom but its most refined expression. Each meditative experience, no matter how profound, must be seen as transient and devoid of inherent value. Attachment to enlightenment, peace, or clarity becomes a new form of bondage. Liberation is only realized when the seeker no longer seeks, and the attained no longer claims to have attained.

**Neutrality of Practice as the Mature Expression**  
Buxiubuzheng describes the absence of dualistic motivation: neither pursuing pleasure nor avoiding pain, neither striving for peace nor resisting disturbance. This is the final unfolding of wisdom, where practice becomes effortless observation of reality as it is — without addition, subtraction, grasping, or rejection. It reflects a mind unshaped by desire or aversion, resting naturally in non-dual awareness.

**Inner Transformation Through Mindful Awareness**  
Real liberation manifests internally as Neizhai zhuanhua — the gradual disappearance of greed, anger, and delusion. This change is not induced by ritual or external authority but by moment-to-moment awareness and the continuous release of clinging. It is visible in the reduction of reactivity, the softening of identity-based defenses, and the arising of equanimity in the face of all conditions.

**Freedom from External Appearances as a Prerequisite**  
Jiefu — the release from attachment to all experiences, however subtle — is necessary for the full realization of Nierhua. Even the most refined meditative joys, if clung to, become obstacles. True liberation requires the total absence of fixation: no identification with peace, no craving for stillness, no preference for any state over another. Only then does the mind rest in unconditioned freedom.

**Liberation as Self-Achieved Through Insight**  
Jietao is not granted by gods, teachers, or rituals. It arises solely from direct insight into suffering, impermanence, and non-self. When wisdom fully realizes these truths, ignorance is extinguished, craving ceases, and the cycle of rebirth ends. No intermediate force or power intervenes; the liberation is the natural consequence of seeing reality as it is — without distortion, resistance, or attachment.
