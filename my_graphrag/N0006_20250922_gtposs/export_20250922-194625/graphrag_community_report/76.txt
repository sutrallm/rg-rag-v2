**Five Upper Hindrances: Composition, Impact, and Remedies in Buddhist Psychology**

**Executive Summary**  
The passage details the five upper hindrances—色相, 無色相, 悔, 断, 無明—identified as foundational root knots that block progress toward liberation. Multiple terms describe these components (e.g., 色罪, 無色茶, 捉遉), reflecting varied cultural or doctrinal emphasis. Ignorance is highlighted not only as a constituent but also as the driving force behind all action. The practice of 四正勤 (Four Right Effort) is presented as the primary method for overcoming these obstacles. Monastic instruction underscores the importance of recognizing and addressing the five upper hindrances for spiritual development.

---

### Enumeration of the Five Upper Hindrances  
The core set is explicitly listed as 色相 (color desire), 無色相 (non‑color desire), 悔 (remorse), 断 (discontinuation), and 無明 (ignorance). Additional references—色罪, 無色茶, 捉遉, and 太—correspond to these same elements, indicating multiple appellations used across texts.

### Repeated Terminology Reflecting Multivalent Descriptions  
Several terms (e.g., 色罪, 無色茶, 捉遉) appear alongside the primary list, suggesting that different traditions or texts use synonymous names to convey the same underlying psychological blocks. This redundancy highlights the widespread recognition of these hindrances across Buddhist literature.

### Dual Role of Ignorance as Component and Catalytic Cause  
Ignorance (無明) is described both as one of the five upper hindrances and as the root cause of all action (行). This duality emphasizes its centrality: it underlies each hindrance and drives the behaviors that sustain them, making its elimination crucial for progress.

### Recommended Practice: Four Right Effort (四正勤)  
The text recommends 四正勤 as the method for overcoming the five upper hindrances. This indicates a practical framework—focused, disciplined effort—that aligns with the analytical understanding of the obstacles.

### Instruction to Monks on Hindrances (諸比丘)  
Monastics are explicitly notified of the existence and composition of the five upper hindrances, signaling that doctrinal teaching places this knowledge at the core of monastic training. Understanding and addressing these obstacles is portrayed as essential for spiritual attainment.
