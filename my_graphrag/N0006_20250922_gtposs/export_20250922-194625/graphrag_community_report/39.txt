**Title**  
Buddha’s Teaching Landscape: Locations, Audiences, and Core Themes  

---  

### Executive Summary  
The Buddha’s discourse record shows a pattern of teaching in a variety of natural and built settings—most notably the East Garden (東園) of Śāmalā, bamboo forests (竹林), and the mountain near King Saha’s city. These venues served diverse audiences: ordained monks and nuns, lay merchants and kings, and even supernatural figures. The sermons consistently address the impermanent world (無常), the boundless nature of reality (不動), and the dangers of attachment. Key disciples—including Mahākasyapa, Gu Shou Sharlif, and 具壽 賓頭盧—appear repeatedly, indicating a stable teacher‑student network. Together, these patterns illuminate how the Buddha leveraged multiple contexts to communicate foundational Buddhist doctrines.

---  

## Buddha’s Distribution of Teaching Sites  
The text notes specific venues where the Buddha spoke:  
- **鹿子母堂** located inside the East Garden (東園) of Śāmalā.  
- **竹林** and the bamboo‑forest gardens in King Saha’s city.  
- **辯才峰** (a mountain peak) and the nearby **祇樹林之給孤獨園**.  
- **王舍城** and the harbor lakes surrounding the royal palace.  
- **Narada (Nārada)**, a garden within **法岳羢国**.  
These sites are repeatedly mentioned as central settings for sermons, healing encounters, and moral instruction.

## Diverse Audiences of the Discourses  
The Buddha addressed a wide spectrum of listeners:  
- Ordained monks (**比丘**) and nuns (**比丘尼**) collecting at gardens and forests.  
- Wealthy merchants, such as a cloth seller in Narada, who also entered the teaching dialogue.  
- Royal figures, including a divine king of Svāhā and King Saha, do the one‑vehicle conversation alongside regional rulers.  
- Spiritual figures, such as the demon 壢岸 and the pair of celestial beings who converse at night.  
These varied groups reveal how the Buddha adapted his messages to different social roles.

## Core Doctrinal Themes Repeated Across Sermons  
Several doctrines recur throughout the record:  
- **Four Mindfulness Practices (四念處)** and the **Five Covers (五蓋)** describing unwholesome mental states.  
- **Five Sensual Pleasures (五妙欲)** and their perils.  
- The **seven bodily components** and the **six aggregates (skandhas)**, each discussed in terms of impermanence and pain.  
- The distinction between the changing world (變易) and the fixed, unmoving reality (不動, 不轉變).  
- The practice of the **four right efforts (四正勤)** and the cultivation of the **five roots (五根)**.  
These teachings consistently framed suffering as rooted in attachment and ignorance.

## Use of Everyday Contexts to Illustrate Spiritual Points  
The Buddha repeatedly invoked familiar settings to ground philosophical ideas:  
- A merchant’s cloth‑selling spot in Narada signals the mundane world’s transient commerce.  
- The demon 壢岸 personifies the bondage of attachment, warning that “attachment binds one to the demon.”  
- The riverbank beside **東河** serves as a backdrop for discussing faith, effort, and concentration roots.  
These contextual anchors help present abstract doctrines in concrete, relatable imagery.

## Teacher‑Disciple Relationships and Continuity  
The text records interactions that reinforce the lineage:  
- **Mahākasyapa** remains solitary after the Buddha’s passing, signifying a continuity of the discipleship model.  
- **Gu Shou Sharlif** and **Gu Shou Uṭṭhigā** receive direct instruction and attain arahantship, illustrating the teacher–student link.  
- **具壽 賓頭盧** and **具壽 阿難** repeatedly engage with the Buddha in healing, guidance, and worship.  
- **Shéng** receives instruction on the Four Mindfulnesss, highlighting the personal mentor–student dynamic.  
These relationships depict a robust teaching community where knowledge is transmitted and preserved.

## Attachment, Dangers, and the Role of Demons  
In the narrative, attachment is repeatedly linked to malice and harm:  
- The demon 壢岸 casts spells to derail the Buddha’s final liberation.  
- The Buddha explains that avoiding attachment frees one from bondage to both 壢岸 and an unnamed entity (䖔).  
- The concept of “lack of mutual compulsion (不相互逼迫)” underscores the importance of independence from external influences.  
These passages present attachment as the root of suffering the Buddha seeks to alleviate.
