**Title**  
Buddha–Disciple Interactions and Sensory Impermanence in Early Discourses  

**Executive Summary**  
The text reveals a tightly organized pattern of teaching that centers on the city of Śāvatthika and its surrounding sites. The Buddha repeatedly addresses a diverse group of disciples—such as Rōgharrā, Gu Shou Chandā, and Gu Shou Uṭṭhigā—who pose detailed questions about the six sense bases, the five aggregates, and the nature of aversion. Each discussion emphasizes the impermanent and painful character of sensory contact, underscoring a core doctrinal theme. The Buddha also engages with local noble figures and supernatural beings, extending the reach of his instruction beyond the monastic community. This structure provides insight into the methods and focal points of early Buddhist pedagogy.

---

### Teaching Venue Concentration in Śāvatthika City  
- Śāvatthika (Sāvatthika) City, including its East Garden, East River, and prominent temples such as the Deer‑Mother Hall, repeatedly appears as the setting for sermons and dialogues.  
- The Deer‑Mother Hall lies within the East Garden and is a frequent location where the Buddha delivers teachings and receives salutations from monks.  
- Additional sites—such as Gurava Garden, the Bamboo‑Forest Garden, and the hill of Ekarāḍha—serve as extended teaching venues, indicating a network of diverse instructional spaces within a single city.

### Question–Answer Seminars with Disciples  
- Multiple disciples request explanations of specific mental and sensory processes.  
- Rōgharrā asks about the nature of the aggregates *sensation (ūpaśyana)*, *thought (saṅkhāra)*, *color (cittā)*, *action (kamma)*, *consciousness (viññāṇa)*, and *intention (manas)*.  
- Gu Shou Chandā, Gu Shou Uṭṭhigā, and other senior monks similarly direct inquiries toward the Buddha, reflecting a systematic approach of clarifying doctrine through direct questioning.  
- The Buddha’s responses cover the impermanent and suffering aspects of each topic, reinforcing key doctrinal points through repeated dialogue.

### Sensory Impermanence and Aversions  
- The six sense bases—*sight*, *ear*, *nose*, *tongue*, *body*, and *intention*—are defined as *impermanent* and *painful*.  
- For every sense base, aversion (*exclusion of*, *dislike*) is described as arising from contact with unfavorable stimuli.  
- The Buddha explicitly teaches that sensory contact generates *feeling (ūpaśyana)*, leading to aversion, and that these phenomena are bound by impermanence.  
- This emphasis illustrates a core method for demonstrating the causes of suffering and the path to liberation.

### Focus on the Five Aggregates and Effort  
- The doctrine of the five aggregates—*form*, *feeling*, *thought*, *action*, and *consciousness*—is repeatedly examined, with the Buddha noting each aggregate’s *impermanent* and *painful* qualities.  
- In several dialogues, the Buddha explains *the four great efforts*, advising monks to cut off desire and greed.  
- The teachings on the *five sensual pleasures* and *five covers* (unwholesome mental states) further outline the unwholesome cycle that teachings aim to transcend.

### Interaction with Divine Figures and Local Nobles  
- The Buddha converses with divine personalities such as *Shiva (Śiva) Yassa* on the mountain of Līṅkura and with *Yassa Yadhura* near the Wang‑Sakha mountain.  
- Human nobles, including *King Dìpō* and *King Saha*, appear in the narrative, indicating that instruction reached beyond monastic circles to royal audiences.  
- These interactions underscore the breadth of the Buddha’s instruction, spanning monastic, noble, and supernatural audiences.

### Cross‑Regional Pedagogy Across Kingdoms  
- Discourses occur not only in Śāvatthika but also in other kingdoms—*Barā*, *Po­ra‑na*, *Bārā*, *Sāvatthika* islands, and the city of *Vijeshara*—demonstrating wide geographic dissemination.  
- Monks such as Gu Shou Chandā, Gu Shou Uṭṭhigā, and Nāgas from Po­ra‑na travel to various locales to engage with the Buddha, indicating a portable form of instruction.  
- The presence of specific semi‑urban or rural teaching sites (e.g., “Narada garden” and “Biraha village”) illustrates that teachings were also delivered in more remote settings.
